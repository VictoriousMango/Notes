Optical Fiber Waveguides & Modes

Step-index vs graded-index fibers, V-number, mode classification and basic dispersion concepts arising from guided modes.

Waveguide

Step-Index Fiber Structure

An optical fiber is a cylindrical dielectric waveguide with a higher-index core surrounded by a lower-index cladding.

  • Core index n₁, cladding index n₂ (n₁ > n₂).
  • Light guided by total internal reflection at core–cladding interface.
  • Numerical aperture: NA ≈ √(n₁² − n₂²).

V-number

Normalized Frequency (V-Number)

V-number determines how many modes a step-index fiber supports.

V = (2π a / λ) · NA
  • a: core radius; λ: operating wavelength.
  • Single-mode condition: V < 2.405 (fundamental HE₁₁ mode only).
  • Number of guided modes (approx, step-index): M ≈ V² / 2.

GI fibers

Graded-Index (GI) Fibers

In GI fibers, the core index decreases gradually from center to edge.

  • Reduces intermodal dispersion by equalizing group delay of modes.
  • Approximate parabolic index profile → sinusoidal ray paths.
  • Used in multimode communication links with improved bandwidth.

Propagation Characteristics & Losses

Attenuation mechanisms, dispersion types and their impact on bit rate and transmission distance.

Loss

Fiber Attenuation Mechanisms

Optical power decays exponentially along the fiber:

P(z) = P(0) e−αz
  • Intrinsic absorption (material), extrinsic absorption (impurities).
  • Rayleigh scattering (∝ 1/λ⁴) dominant in low-loss region.
  • Bending loss (macro & micro-bending) and connector/splice losses.

Dispersion

Types of Dispersion in Fibers

Pulse broadening arises from different components arriving at different times.

  • Material dispersion: n(λ) variation in core.
  • Waveguide dispersion: mode field distribution vs λ.
  • Intermodal dispersion: different modes take different paths (multimode fiber).

Bandwidth

Bit Rate–Distance Product

Dispersion limits how fast data can be sent over a given length without excessive intersymbol interference.

  • For a given dispersion, there is a maximum B·L product.
  • Dispersion compensation (DCF, Bragg gratings, etc.) extends link length.

Optical Transmitters & Modulation

Laser/LED transmitters and how information is impressed on the optical carrier.

Sources

LED vs Laser Diode Transmitters

Both are used as optical sources, but with very different properties.

  • LEDs: broad spectrum, low coherence, simple drive, lower cost.
  • Laser diodes: narrow linewidth, high power, high coupling efficiency.
  • Choice depends on required distance, bit rate and cost.

Modulation types

Direct & External Modulation

Information can modulate the laser itself or an external device.

  • Direct modulation: drive the injection current → IM of output power.
  • External modulation: use LiNbO₃ Mach–Zehnder or electro-absorption modulators.
  • External modulation reduces chirp and improves high-speed performance.

Formats

Basic Digital Modulation Formats

Modulation formats map bits onto optical amplitude/phase.

  • OOK (On–Off Keying): simplest, intensity-based.
  • NRZ vs RZ pulse shapes affect spectral width and dispersion tolerance.
  • Advanced formats: DPSK, QPSK, QAM used in high-capacity systems.

Receivers, Noise & Capacity

Photodetectors, receiver front ends, noise sources and Shannon capacity.

Detector

Photodiodes in Optical Receivers

PIN and avalanche photodiodes convert optical signals to electrical current.

  • PIN: high speed, moderate gain, low noise.
  • APD: internal gain via impact ionization, higher sensitivity with extra noise.
  • Bandwidth set by junction capacitance and load.

Noise

Key Noise Sources in Optical Receivers

Noise limits minimum detectable power and BER.

  • Shot noise: due to discrete nature of electrons/photons.
  • Thermal (Johnson) noise: from resistive elements.
  • Amplifier noise: from transimpedance and post-amps.

Capacity

Shannon Capacity & Optical Links

Even with ideal coding, capacity is limited by SNR and bandwidth.

C = B · log₂(1 + S/N)
  • B: electrical bandwidth; S/N: signal-to-noise ratio at receiver.
  • Higher launch power helps until nonlinear effects and noise dominate.
  • Sets upper bound on bit rate for given link conditions.

Optical Amplifiers & Nonlinear Effects

EDFAs and other amplifiers for long-haul links, plus Kerr and other nonlinear phenomena in fibers at high powers.

EDFA

Erbium-Doped Fiber Amplifier (EDFA)

A length of erbium-doped fiber pumped by a laser to amplify signals around 1550 nm.

  • Acts as a quasi-flat-gain amplifier over the C/L bands.
  • Key parameters: gain, noise figure, saturation power.
  • Enables multi-span, long-distance WDM systems.

Kerr

Nonlinear Effects in Fibers

High intensities in long fibers cause nonlinear interactions.

  • SPM (Self-Phase Modulation): intensity-dependent phase → spectral broadening.
  • XPM (Cross-Phase Modulation): one channel modulates phase of another.
  • FWM (Four-Wave Mixing): generates new frequencies, relevant in dense WDM.

Trade-offs

Power vs Nonlinearity Trade-off

Raising power improves SNR but increases nonlinear distortions.

  • Optimal launch power balances ASE noise and nonlinear penalties.
  • Dispersion management affects effective nonlinear length.

WDM / DWDM & Link Design

Using many wavelengths on the same fiber, multiplexing components and system-level power budgeting.

Concept

WDM & DWDM Basics

Wavelength Division Multiplexing sends multiple independent channels at different wavelengths over a single fiber.

  • WDM: channel spacing ~nm; DWDM: very narrow spacing (0.8 nm / 100 GHz or less).
  • Requires precise wavelength control and filters.
  • Massively increases total capacity of a single fiber.

Components

Key WDM Components

Passive and active components manage many wavelengths.

  • Multiplexers/Demultiplexers (AWGs, thin-film filters).
  • Optical add-drop multiplexers (OADMs, ROADMs).
  • Wavelength-selective switches in reconfigurable networks.

Link budget

Optical Link Power Budget

Ensures that received power is above sensitivity after accounting for all losses.

  • Budget includes fiber loss, connectors, splices, and margins.
  • Amplifiers used to restore power at intermediate points.
  • Combined with dispersion and OSNR analysis for full system design.

Free-Space Optics & Integrated Waveguides

Atmospheric free-space links and planar/integrated photonic circuits as guided-wave systems in different platforms.

FSO

Free-Space Optical (FSO) Communication

Uses laser beams propagating through atmosphere instead of fiber.

  • Advantages: high data rate, license-free spectrum, rapid deployment.
  • Limitations: weather dependence (fog, rain, turbulence), pointing and tracking.
  • Used for last-mile, building-to-building links, backhaul.

Integrated

Integrated Optical Waveguides

Planar lightwave circuits (PLCs) and photonic integrated circuits guide light on a chip using high-index-contrast waveguides.

  • Platforms: Si, SiN, InP, LiNbO₃, etc.
  • Components: splitters, couplers, MZIs, filters, modulators, detectors.
  • Enable compact, low-cost, high-volume optical systems.

Coupling

Fiber–Chip Coupling

Efficiently getting light between fiber and integrated waveguide is critical.

  • Edge coupling (butt-coupling) vs grating couplers (vertical).
  • Mode size mismatch causes coupling loss; tapers used to adapt modes.
