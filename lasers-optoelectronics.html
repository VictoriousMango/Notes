Laser Basics & Rate Equations

Stimulated emission, population inversion, gain and simple rate equation picture of laser operation.

Principle

Conditions for Laser Action

LASER = Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation.

  • Stimulated emission coefficient > absorption in active region → net gain.
  • Population inversion: N₂ > N₁ between upper and lower laser levels.
  • Optical feedback: resonator provides multiple passes through gain medium.

Einstein picture

Absorption, Spontaneous & Stimulated Emission

Three fundamental radiative processes described by Einstein coefficients.

  • Absorption: lower → upper level by photon absorption.
  • Spontaneous emission: random-phase photon, isotropic directions.
  • Stimulated emission: incident photon triggers identical photon (same ν, k, pol.).

Rate model

Simple Laser Rate Equations (Conceptual)

Rate equations track evolution of population inversion and photon number.

  • dN/dt = pump − decay − stimulated emission term.
  • dS/dt = gain · S − losses + spontaneous emission term (β).
  • Steady state above threshold → constant N ≈ Nthr, output power ∝ (pump − threshold).

Laser Resonators & Beam Properties

Optical cavities, longitudinal/transverse modes, Gaussian beams and quality factor.

Cavity

Optical Resonator Basics

Mirrors form a cavity that selects certain standing-wave field patterns (modes).

  • Resonance condition: round-trip phase shift = 2πm.
  • Longitudinal modes: discrete frequencies separated by Δν = c / (2nL).
  • Transverse modes: TEMmn patterns determined by mirror geometry.

Stability

Resonator Stability Condition

Only certain mirror curvatures and separations form stable cavities.

  • Parameters: g₁ = 1 − L/R₁, g₂ = 1 − L/R₂.
  • Stability: 0 < g₁g₂ < 1.
  • Stable cavity supports bounded Gaussian-like modes.

Beam quality

Gaussian Beam & M² Factor

Ideal fundamental laser mode is Gaussian; real beams deviate from this.

  • Waist w₀, Rayleigh range zR, divergence θ ~ λ/(πw₀).
  • M² >= 1 quantifies beam quality (1 for ideal Gaussian).
  • Lower M² → better focusability and higher irradiance at focus.

Semiconductor Basics & Junctions

Energy bands, carriers, p–n junctions and radiative recombination fundamentals.

Bands

Band Structure & Bandgap

In semiconductors, allowed energy levels form bands separated by a bandgap Eg.

  • Valence band: mostly filled electrons; conduction band: mobile electrons.
  • Direct bandgap: k-conserving transitions → efficient light emission (e.g., GaAs).
  • Indirect bandgap: needs phonon; weak radiative recombination (e.g., Si).

p–n junction

p–n Junction Under Bias

Basic building block for LEDs, laser diodes and photodiodes.

  • At equilibrium: depletion region, built-in potential.
  • Forward bias: barrier reduced → injection of carriers across junction.
  • Recombination in active region can be radiative (light) or non-radiative.

Recombination

Radiative vs Non-Radiative Recombination

When an electron recombines with a hole, energy can appear as photon or heat.

  • Radiative: band-to-band (LED/LD emission). Photon energy ≈ Eg.
  • Non-radiative: via defects (Shockley–Read–Hall) or Auger processes.
  • Quantum efficiency depends on ratio of these rates.

LEDs & Laser Diodes

Structures, emission characteristics and main comparison points between LEDs and semiconductor lasers.

LED

Light Emitting Diodes (LEDs)

Spontaneous emission from a forward-biased p–n junction in a direct bandgap semiconductor.

  • Broad spectral width (tens of nm), Lambertian emission pattern.
  • Lower brightness & lower coupling efficiency into fibers.
  • Advantages: low cost, simple drive, excellent reliability.

LD

Laser Diodes (LDs)

Add an optical cavity to the LED structure → stimulated emission dominates above threshold.

  • Active region in double heterostructure or quantum well.
  • Facet reflections form Fabry–Pérot cavity; or gratings for DFB/DBR lasers.
  • Narrow linewidth, high brightness, efficient coupling to fibers/waveguides.

Threshold

Laser Diode L–I Curve & Threshold

Output vs current shows a kink at threshold current Ith.

  • Below Ith: mainly spontaneous emission (LED-like region).
  • Above Ith: stimulated emission dominates, linear slope efficiency.
  • Temperature strongly affects Ith and efficiency.

Photodetectors & Receiver Front-End

PIN and APD structures, responsivity and basic receiver considerations (overlaps with guided-wave optics but from device perspective).

PIN

PIN Photodiode

p–i–n structure provides depletion region where photons are absorbed and electron–hole pairs are generated.

  • Reverse bias extends depletion width and speeds collection.
  • Responsivity R ≈ η q / (hν) [A/W].
  • Fast response, low dark current → widely used in receivers.

APD

Avalanche Photodiode (APD)

APD provides internal gain via impact ionization in a high-field region.

  • Multiplication factor M > 1 amplifies photocurrent.
  • Improves sensitivity but adds excess noise and requires high bias.
  • Used when very low received power and high speed are needed.

Front-end

Transimpedance Amplifier (TIA) Front-End

Converts detector current to voltage while controlling noise bandwidth.

  • Feedback resistor sets gain; capacitances set bandwidth.
  • Trade-off between gain and speed.
  • Total input-referred noise dictates minimum detectable signal.

Modulation, Linewidth & Noise

How lasers are modulated, what sets linewidth and what main noise processes appear.

IM

Intensity Modulation of Laser Diodes

Varying injection current varies output optical power.

  • Small-signal modulation: relaxation oscillation frequency sets speed limit.
  • Large-signal: pattern effects and chirp may degrade transmission in fibers.
  • Direct IM simplest but not always best for high-speed links.

Linewidth

Laser Linewidth & Schawlow–Townes

Even “monochromatic” lasers have finite linewidth due to phase noise.

  • Fundamental limit: Schawlow–Townes formula (inversely ∝ output power, Q²).
  • Semiconductor lasers also have linewidth enhancement factor (α-factor).
  • Narrow linewidth crucial for coherent communication and sensing.

Noise

Key Noise Mechanisms in Laser Output

Fluctuations in amplitude and phase originate from various processes.

  • Relative intensity noise (RIN) from spontaneous emission fluctuations.
  • Phase noise → linewidth; important for coherent detection.
  • Technical noise: drive current fluctuations, thermal drift, mechanical vibrations.

Applications & Advanced Concepts

A quick view of where these devices are used and a few advanced structures you might be asked about at a conceptual level.

Comms

Lasers & Detectors in Fiber Links

Core building blocks for long-distance, high-capacity communication.

  • DFB / DBR lasers at 1310/1550 nm for WDM systems.
  • PIN/APD receivers with TIAs for high bit rates.
  • Design must balance power, dispersion and nonlinearities (see GWO sheet).

Sensing

Optoelectronic Devices in Sensing

Many sensors use lasers + detectors as transducers.

  • LIDAR: pulsed lasers + timing electronics → distance / 3D imaging.
  • Interferometric sensors: phase shifts encode displacement, pressure, etc.
  • Photodiode arrays & cameras used for imaging-based metrology.

Advanced

Advanced Semiconductor Laser Structures (Conceptual)

Device design can strongly tailor spectrum, direction and efficiency.

  • DFB/DBR lasers: built-in gratings for single-frequency emission.
  • VCSELs: vertical-cavity surface-emitting lasers, circular beams, easy 2D arrays.
  • Quantum well / quantum dot lasers: improved performance, lower threshold, tailored gain.
