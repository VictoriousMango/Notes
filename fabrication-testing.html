Fabrication & Testing of Optical Components

Think of this as the life cycle of an optic: choose material → rough/finish shape → polish/figure → test → iterate until specs are met → integrate into system. These cards are designed so that you can describe each stage in a clean, exam-friendly way.

Optical Materials & Glass Properties

Glass types, optical constants, dispersion and material selection criteria for lenses and mirrors.

Basics

Key Optical Material Parameters

Choice of material is driven by both optical and mechanical properties.

  • Refractive index n(λ) and dispersion (Abbe number, partial dispersions).
  • Transmission window (UV–VIS–IR), absorption bands.
  • Mechanical/thermal: hardness, CTE, thermal conductivity, density.

Glasses

Crown vs Flint Glasses

Traditional classification of optical glasses based on index and dispersion.

  • Crown: lower n, higher Abbe number (low dispersion).
  • Flint: higher n, lower Abbe number (high dispersion).
  • Achromats combine one crown + one flint to control chromatic aberration.

Crystals & ceramics

Crystalline & Ceramic Optical Materials

Beyond standard glasses, special materials are used for IR, high power or special functions.

  • Crystals: CaF₂, Si, Ge, ZnSe → IR optics, low dispersion, laser windows.
  • Ceramics: transparent Al₂O₃, spinel → high strength, harsh environments.
  • Coatings and surface treatment strongly affect final performance.

Fabrication of Optical Components & Elements

From raw blanks to ground, centered and edged lenses/mirrors and structured elements.

Sequence

Typical Lens Fabrication Flow

Most classical lens fabrication follows a repeatable chain of operations.

  • Blank preparation (cutting from melt or stock, blocking).
  • Generating: rough shaping of curvature (curve generator, grinding wheel).
  • Fine grinding → polishing → centering/edging → coating → final inspection.

Generating

Generating & Fine Grinding

Generating establishes approx. surface shape; fine grinding reduces subsurface damage.

  • Curve generator uses tool motion to approximate desired radius.
  • Abrasives: coarse SiC → finer Al₂O₃/diamond → reduces roughness.
  • Goal: leave enough material for polishing while controlling form.

Special elements

Fabrication of Aspheres & Freeforms (Concept)

Non-spherical and freeform optics often need deterministic machining.

  • Single-point diamond turning for IR metals/crystals.
  • Sub-aperture polishing, magnetorheological finishing for glass aspheres.
  • Metrology drives the figuring loop (measure → correct → remeasure).

Polishing Technologies & Computer-Controlled Polishing

Classical pitch polishing, modern sub-aperture tools and computer-controlled figuring for high-precision surfaces.

Classical

Pitch Polishing Basics

Classical lens polishing uses a compliant pitch tool and slurry of fine abrasives.

  • Pitch conforms to surface; motion averages tool marks → smooth surface.
  • Material removal rate described by Preston’s law: Δh ∝ p · v · t.
  • Well suited for spherical surfaces, moderate accuracy.

Sub-aperture

Sub-Aperture Polishing Tools

Small tools polish local regions and are scanned over the surface.

  • Examples: bonnet polishing, small lap tools, MRF heads.
  • Enable local correction of residuals from interferometric map.
  • Key for aspheres, freeforms and large optics with tight specs.

CCP / MRF

Computer-Controlled Polishing (CCP) & MRF

Deterministic process: removal function + dwell time map → controlled figuring.

  • Removal function measured for given tool & conditions.
  • Dwell time distribution computed to reduce measured error map.
  • MRF (Magnetorheological Finishing) uses magnetically stiffened slurry for highly repeatable removal.

Surface & Form Testing / Interferometry

How we measure surface figure, roughness and mid-spatial error using interferometers and profilometers.

Interferometry

Basic Interferometry Concepts

Interferometers compare test wavefront with a reference to reveal phase errors.

  • Fringes represent contours of equal optical path difference (OPD).
  • Fringe spacing related to slope; curvature leads to fringe curvature.
  • Common setups: Fizeau, Twyman–Green, Mach–Zehnder, Michelson.

Surface figure

Testing Surface Figure with Fizeau Interferometer

Test surface is placed against a high-quality reference flat or sphere.

  • Reference and test surfaces form cavity → interference pattern.
  • Deviations of fringes from straight/equally spaced indicate figure error.
  • Phase-shifting interferometry quantifies wavefront error numerically.

Roughness

Surface Roughness & Scatter

Very fine-scale surface errors cause scatter and reduce contrast.

  • Measured using profilometers (contact) or white-light interferometers.
  • RMS roughness and PSD (power spectral density) describe surface statistics.
  • High spatial-frequency errors → veiling glare, stray light issues.

Testing of Lenses, Mirrors & Optical Systems

From single-element tests to full-system evaluation: autocollimation, null tests and imaging metrics.

Autocollimation

Autocollimation Tests

A collimated beam reflects off a flat mirror at focus to test lens performance.

  • Source at lens focus → collimated beam.
  • Flat mirror returns beam back through lens → examine focus quality.
  • Used to test focal length, aberrations and alignment.

Null testing

Null Tests for Aspheric Surfaces

Optical arrangement designed so that ideal surface produces “null” straight fringes.

  • Use refractive/reflective null optics (computer-generated holograms, compensators).
  • Any residual fringes directly represent aspheric figure error.
  • Standard approach for high-precision aspheres and telescopes.

System metrics

System-Level Performance Metrics

Final system tests connect fabrication errors to imaging quality.

  • MTF/PSF measurements: resolution and contrast.
  • Line-of-sight, boresight alignment, field curvature tests.
  • Environmental tests: temperature cycling, vibration and shock.

Large Optical Mirrors & Industrial / Practical Aspects

Special challenges in fabricating large optics and how real-world constraints (cost, schedule, manufacturability) affect design choices.

Large mirrors

Challenges in Large Telescope Mirrors

As size increases, mechanical, thermal and metrology issues quickly dominate.

  • Gravity-induced sag: need lightweighted structures and active supports.
  • Thermal gradients cause figure change; low-CTE materials (Zerodur, ULE).
  • Segmented mirrors require precise phasing and edge control.

Manufacturability

Design for Manufacturability (DFM) in Optics

Optical design must consider how surfaces will actually be made and tested.

  • Avoid unnecessarily extreme aspheric departures where possible.
  • Use common radii, wedges and glass types to reduce cost.
  • Specify realistic tolerances consistent with tooling and metrology.

Industrial view

Industrial Process Flow & Quality Control

Production optics require stable, repeatable processes with QC gates.

  • Incoming material inspection → in-process metrology → final acceptance tests.
  • Process control charts monitor removal rates and defect levels.
  • Documentation: traveler sheets, inspection reports, test certificates.

Advanced / Research-Level Topics

Emerging polishing methods, structured surfaces and next-generation metrology ideas.

Structured optics

Diffractive & Meta-Optical Surfaces (Fabrication View)

Fine micro/nano-structured patterns replace traditional curvature to control phase.

  • Fabrication via lithography, etching, nanoimprint or multi-level machining.
  • Metrology requires profilometry and scatter measurements, not just interferometry.
  • Very sensitive to fabrication tolerances and contamination.

On-machine

On-Machine Metrology & Feedback

Measurement integrated into fabrication machine to shorten iteration loops.

  • In-situ interferometry, tactile probes or optical sensors on machine axes.
  • Closed-loop control: measure → compute correction → cut/polish → remeasure.

Automation

Automation & Data-Driven Fabrication

Modern shops use automation and data analytics to stabilize quality.

  • Recipe-based CCP with stored removal functions and machine states.
  • Statistical analysis of run-to-run variation to refine process windows.
  • Links naturally with optical design tolerancing and Monte Carlo analysis.
