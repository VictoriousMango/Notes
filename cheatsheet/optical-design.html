Optical System Design & Analysis

In VIVA, most questions boil down to: “What is the aberration?”, “How does it show up in images or ray fans?”, “How can we reduce it by design choices?” and “How do software tools support this?”. Each card here is tuned to answer exactly that type of question.

Aberrations – Fundamentals & Seidel Terms

Primary (Seidel) aberrations, longitudinal vs transverse errors and how these map to spot shapes and blur in the image plane.

Definition

What is an Aberration?

Aberrations are deviations from ideal (Gaussian) imaging, causing rays from a point object to not focus to a perfect point.

  • Result: blur, distortion or shape changes of point/line images.
  • Wave view: wavefront deviates from ideal spherical wavefront.
  • Ray view: ray intercepts differ from paraxial predictions.

Seidel 5

Seidel Primary Aberrations (Third-Order)

For rotationally symmetric systems, the five classical Seidel aberrations are:

  • Spherical aberration (on-axis, aperture dependent).
  • Coma (off-axis, aperture × field dependent).
  • Astigmatism & field curvature (off-axis, field dependent).
  • Distortion (geometric mapping error, mainly field dependent).

Longitudinal vs transverse

Longitudinal vs Transverse Aberrations

Same aberration can be expressed as focus shift (longitudinal) or lateral error.

  • Longitudinal: Δz along axis; e.g., marginal rays focus closer than paraxial rays.
  • Transverse: lateral intercept error in image plane; relates to blur size.
  • Spot diagrams and ray intercept curves visualize transverse aberrations.

Advanced Aberration Theory & Field Behaviour

Higher-order aberrations, field dependence and how modern design uses wavefront or Zernike descriptions rather than only Seidel terms.

Higher order

Beyond Third Order: Higher-Order Aberrations

Real systems often operate outside strict third-order validity, especially with high NA and wide fields.

  • Aberration contributions from 5th, 7th order terms etc. become important.
  • Classical Seidel picture still useful qualitatively, but quantitative analysis uses full wavefront expansions.

Zernike

Zernike Polynomials for Aberration Description

Wavefront over a circular pupil is often expanded in Zernike polynomials.

  • Orthogonal basis on the unit circle; each term linked to an aberration type.
  • Examples: defocus, astigmatism, coma, trefoil, spherical, etc.
  • Directly used in interferometer wavefront maps and AO systems.

Field behaviour

Field Dependence of Aberrations

Different aberrations grow differently with field angle and pupil coordinate.

  • Spherical: primarily aperture (pupil) dependent; affects on-axis and off-axis.
  • Coma: ∝ field · pupil²; star images turn into “comet” shapes off-axis.
  • Astigmatism/field curvature: field dependent; difference between sagittal & tangential focus.

Ray Tracing & Image Evaluation

How design software uses ray tracing to predict imaging performance and generate spot diagrams, ray fans and MTF curves.

Types of rays

Paraxial vs Real Ray Tracing

Two levels of ray tracing are commonly used.

  • Paraxial: uses small-angle approximations; good for first-order properties (focal length, magnification, principal planes).
  • Real (exact) ray tracing: uses Snell’s law exactly; reveals aberrations.
  • Design tools do real ray tracing by default to evaluate performance.

Diagnostics

Spot Diagrams & Ray Fan Plots

Visual diagnostics used to interpret aberrations in design software.

  • Spot diagram: distribution of intersection points of many rays at image plane.
  • Ray fan: plot of ray intercept vs pupil coordinate (X, Y fans).
  • Distinct patterns correspond to spherical, coma, astigmatism etc.

MTF

MTF-Based Image Quality Evaluation

MTF curves compare system performance to diffraction limit across spatial frequencies.

  • MTF drop indicates loss of contrast at given spatial frequency.
  • Polychromatic MTF accounts for realistic broadband performance.
  • Often used for specification and comparison of lens designs.

Lens Design & Optimization Workflow

From first-order layout to fully optimized design using merit functions and automatic optimization in software.

First-order

First-Order Layout

Start with paraxial analysis to satisfy basic imaging constraints.

  • Given object distance, image distance, F/#, field of view → choose focal lengths.
  • Decide number of elements, types (positive/negative, cemented groups).
  • Place stops and pupils to control vignetting and aberration distribution.

Merit function

Merit Function & Optimization

Design software uses a merit function that quantifies “badness” of a design.

  • Merit function includes contributions from aberrations, MTF, distortion, constraints.
  • Variables: radii, thicknesses, glass types, aspheric coefficients, tilts/decenters.
  • Local or global optimization algorithms search for parameter sets that minimize merit.

Balancing

Aberration Balancing in Multi-Element Lenses

Different elements contribute with opposite signs to the same aberration.

  • Positive elements often introduce spherical and chromatic aberration.
  • Negative elements used to balance these while maintaining focal length.
  • Classic designs (doublets, triplets, Tessar, Petzval) embody such balancing strategies.

Refractive Systems & Lens Classifications

Common refractive lens families, what they’re used for, and which aberrations they are good at controlling.

Singlets & doublets

Singlets, Doublets & Achromats

Small building blocks that appear in larger designs.

  • Singlet: single lens element; limited aberration correction.
  • Achromatic doublet: crown + flint cemented; corrects primary chromatic and some spherical aberration.
  • Apochromats extend color correction using extra elements or special glasses.

Imaging lenses

Classic Imaging Lens Types

Families like Tessar, Petzval, Double-Gauss are standard starting points.

  • Double-Gauss: good for fast, normal focal length lenses (50 mm f/1.4 style).
  • Petzval: wide-aperture, field-curvature limited; basis for some fast lenses.
  • Telephoto & retrofocus: compact long/short systems using negative groups.

Zooms

Zoom Lens Concepts (High Level)

Zooms vary focal length while keeping image plane approximately fixed.

  • Use moving groups: variator, compensator and sometimes relay groups.
  • Maintain focus and aberration correction over zoom range.
  • More variables and constraints → heavy use of optimization and tolerancing tools.

Reflective Systems & Telescopes

Mirror-based systems, their aberrations and common telescope configurations.

Mirrors

Advantages of Mirror Systems

Reflective systems avoid many limitations of lenses, especially for wide spectral ranges.

  • No chromatic aberration from reflection (angle same for all λ at given n).
  • Large apertures practical using lightweight mirrors and segments.
  • Still suffer from spherical, coma, astigmatism etc. depending on shape.

Telescopes

Basic Telescope Types

Common two-mirror designs use combinations of conics.

  • Cassegrain: parabolic primary + hyperbolic secondary → compact long-focus system.
  • Gregorian, Ritchey–Chrétien variants optimize field and aberrations.
  • Field corrector lenses sometimes added near focus for wide-field imaging.

Field correction

Wide-Field Reflective Systems

Advanced telescopes use additional mirrors/lenses to flatten field and control aberrations.

  • Three-mirror anastigmats (TMA) correct spherical, coma and astigmatism over wide field.
  • Off-axis segments avoid central obstruction and ghosting in some designs.

High-NA & Non-Paraxial Systems

Where paraxial approximations break down and vector nature of light and more exact diffraction analysis become important.

Limits

Breakdown of Paraxial Approximation

High numerical aperture or very wide fields require non-paraxial treatment.

  • Large angles → sinθ, tanθ no longer ≈ θ.
  • Aberration behavior differs significantly from third-order theory.
  • Vector diffraction and polarization effects cannot be ignored near focus.

High-NA imaging

High-NA Microscopy Objectives

Objectives with NA > 0.9 in immersion systems push design and manufacturing limits.

  • Complex multi-element designs with aspheres and special glasses.
  • Must correct spherical, coma, astigmatism, field curvature and chromatic aberrations simultaneously.
  • Need accurate modeling of pupil aberrations and vector PSF.

Telecentric

Telecentric Systems

Designed so chief rays are parallel to axis in object or image space.

  • Object-space telecentric: magnification insensitive to object distance (useful in metrology).
  • Image-space telecentric: constant chief-ray angle at sensor, good for imaging onto microlens arrays, etc.

Computational & Practical Design Questions

Tolerancing, Monte Carlo, and the link between design data and real manufactured systems with alignment errors and environment.

Tolerancing

Optical Tolerancing

Tolerancing studies how small deviations in parameters affect performance.

  • Parameters: radii, thickness, decenter, tilt, index, wedge, surface figure.
  • Metric: change in MTF, wavefront error, image shift etc.
  • Results guide manufacturing specs and alignment procedures.

Monte Carlo

Monte Carlo Analysis

Randomized sampling of tolerances simulates a population of manufactured lenses.

  • Each run: random perturbations within tolerance limits.
  • Analyze distribution of performance metrics vs required spec.
  • Helps estimate yield and refine tolerances cost-effectively.

Alignment

Alignment Sensitivity

Some designs are much more sensitive to decenter/tilt than others.

  • Misalignments introduce coma, astigmatism, image shift.
  • Designers prefer configurations with reduced sensitivity for easier assembly.
  • Tolerancing often separates manufacturing errors from alignment errors.

Advanced Design & High-Performance Optics

Aspheres, freeforms, zooms and specialized systems for high-performance imaging and non-imaging optics.

Aspheres

Role of Aspheric Surfaces

Aspheres allow correction of aberrations that would otherwise need many spherical elements.

  • Reduce spherical aberration and improve off-axis behaviour.
  • Useful in fast, compact systems (e.g., smartphone lenses).
  • Trade-off: harder fabrication & metrology, tighter tolerances.

Freeforms

Freeform Optical Surfaces

Freeforms lack rotational symmetry, giving designer more degrees of freedom.

  • Enable compact, off-axis systems with good aberration correction.
  • Heavily used in head-up displays, AR/VR and complex imaging systems.
  • Require advanced design tools and metrology (interferometers with CGHs, stitching profilometers).

Non-imaging

Non-Imaging Optics (Conceptual)

Designed for energy transfer and uniformity, not image formation.

  • Examples: condensers, light pipes, LED collimators, solar concentrators.
  • Figures of merit: efficiency, illuminance uniformity, etendue conservation.
  • Design methods often use ray mapping and optimization distinct from imaging lenses.
